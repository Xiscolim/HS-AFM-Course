AFM artifacts in biological samples

An AFM image can look sharp and detailed and still show features that are not on your sample. This guide summarises a review chapter on where those artifacts come from, how to recognise them, and how to avoid them.

Why artifacts matter in biology

AFM can image proteins, DNA, viruses, tissue and living cells in their native liquid environment, with nanometre resolution. Optical microscopy needs stains for most cells, and electron microscopy usually needs dried samples.

The price is that AFM is invasive, slow, and the image is never a direct photograph of the sample. Every image is a mix of the sample and the measurement itself. In liquid, van der Waals forces are screened, so molecules stick weakly to the substrate and the tip–sample force must be tuned lower than the molecule–substrate force.

1. Artifacts from the tip and cantilever

Tip broadening (convolution)

An AFM image is the convolution of the tip shape with the object. Features always look wider than they are. DNA is about 2 nm across, yet an ordinary tip (r ≈ 10 nm) gives 10–20 nm widths, and ultrasharp tips give 2.7–5 nm. If the tip radius is larger than the gap between two objects, the tip never touches the substrate between them and they merge into one.

Remedies. Use tips with r smaller than the typical feature size (carbon-nanotube or diamond-like needles, r ≈ 1–2 nm). Most ultrasharp tips are too stiff for liquid. You can also deconvolve: estimate the tip with a test grating, or do "blind" reconstruction using dilation and erosion.

Finite cone angle and shadows

The tip is a cone or pyramid, so tall objects (micrometres) get sloped edges, shadows and a faceted look. Objects such as the globular clusters in Fig. 4 look like crystals instead of spheres. For E. coli, shadows run at a fixed angle to the cantilever tilt, regardless of scan direction. Use cantilevers with the highest aspect ratio you can.

Double tips and repeating patterns

A tip with two or more apexes images every feature twice (or more). Telltale signs are triangular features, pairs with identical shape and ghost objects. In the chapter, a new Multi75Al tip showed a perfect 20 nm periodic pattern on AHAPS. It appeared on the first two scans, vanished on later scans, and neither ultrasharp tips on two different AFMs nor SEM saw it. The conclusion was a tip artifact.

First checks for a suspected artifact: change scan size and position, rotate the scan direction, repeat with a fresh tip, and confirm with a second tip type or another technique.

Tip damage, collisions and phase reversal

A collision can blunt the tip, or pick up particles that change the tip's mass and surface chemistry. Topography may barely change, but the phase image can shift strongly or flip sign. The chapter shows phase contrast reversing mid-scan on three different samples.

Remedies. Avoid sharp or hard features if you can. Calibrate phase against a standard object before and after the experiment. Keep the same tip and watch for changes. Diamond or diamond-coated tips resist wear.

Laser and optical lever

Detector signal versus real deflection is nonlinear (the shape of the laser spot is the main cause). On bright, reflective samples a large laser spot can create interference fringes. Realign the laser, or use a low-coherence source.

2. Artifacts from the scanner

  • Creep and drift. Right after approach, piezo creep changes the oscillation amplitude. Wait up to about 30 minutes for equilibrium. Thermal drift and hysteresis remain even with closed-loop scanners.
  • Tube bending. A piezotube bends as it scans, giving z errors and curved backgrounds. Flat samples can be levelled by software. Patterned samples with tall features cannot be corrected reliably.
  • Feedback gain (FB) too high. The scanner oscillates and produces periodic, sometimes audible noise, often on steep slopes.
  • Feedback gain too low. The tip cannot track the surface. Features smear, and shapes change with scan direction.
  • Closed loop. It can add its own oscillations. Switch it off if it prevents a usable gain.

3. Artifacts from the sample

  • Drift even when well attached. Stabilise temperature, or scan faster.
  • Too sticky or too soft. The tip makes multiple contact points. This is a big problem for cells.
  • Non-conductive, charged samples. Stored charge creates long-range forces, so the image may not be topography. Kelvin probe images may not match the true charge distribution either. Grounding both conductive and insulating samples usually helps.

4. Artifacts from the tip–sample interaction

Contrast reversal (attractive vs repulsive regime)

In TM the cantilever switches between attractive and repulsive regimes. When it flips mid-scan you see strange contours, unexpected height shifts and changing resolution. Objects can even look like holes. It depends on SP/SP₀, which may differ across the image, so the artifact may appear in only part of it.

Use a lower SP, or try contact-resonance imaging.

Tip contamination and drifting SP₀

In liquid, soft material sticks to the tip. The tip gets heavier, the free amplitude A₀ drops, and SP (= A/A₀) rises until it reaches SP₀. Contact is then lost, the topography blurs and phase contrast disappears. Re-tune and adjust SP whenever SP₀ changes. A tip modified with a thiol, or with debris, gives unstable amplitude and noisy images.

Dragged debris

Loose particles pushed by the tip leave diagonal tracks that look like surface features. Glitches in the feedback signal each time the particle is dragged are the clue. The tracks may show up only in phase.

Flying tip

The feedback loop cannot keep up, so the tip loses the surface. Raise gain, lower SP and scan slower. For weakly attached cells or soft protein layers there is often no easy fix. Electrostatic attraction can also be the cause.

Tapping-mode nanoshaving

Large amplitude or a stiff cantilever can sweep away soft layers, so the layer looks thinner or a hole looks "filled". Nanoshaving works by imaging first in contact mode (to cut a hole) and then in TM. Keep scanning in TM, and the layer gradually shrinks. Check with a larger overview scan. To measure layer height, use one TM scan with small amplitude.

Height is underestimated, width is overestimated

Soft objects are compressed by the tip. DNA is about 2 nm tall but measures 0.7–1.2 nm in TM, and 1.7 nm in jumping mode. A stiffer cantilever and a lower SP compress more. Non-contact and jumping modes are gentler but technically harder.

5. Moiré: interference with the scan raster

A regular surface pattern can interfere with the scan raster and produce a wave pattern that is not on the sample. Check it by changing the scan direction and setpoint. If the waves rotate or change, it is moiré.

How to avoid artifacts

Approach carefully

A crash on approach can blunt the tip even in TM. If the amplitude drops to zero, or the feedback is too high so the tip oscillates on contact, damage follows. The force is roughly that of contact mode, around 600 nN for NSG cantilevers.

  1. Do a fast approach with SP > SP₀, until the cantilever feels the surface.
  2. Re-tune amplitude and frequency.
  3. Approach again at the final setpoint and lower speed, in "safe" mode (the software checks the setpoint after each motor step).
  4. Do not approach in contact mode.

The first images after approach are the best the tip will give. Take high-resolution scans first and the large overview later. Scan positions near a boundary (such as H/O) may be hard to find later, so keep them away from it.

The energy a stiffer cantilever puts in per cycle is larger (E = ½kA²). In liquid Q is usually below 10, so energy loss per cycle is larger than in air. For k ≈ 3 N/m and A ≈ 60 nm, E is about 10⁻¹⁵ J, enough to push an FBS layer or a weakly attached cell.

Amplitude, setpoint and feedback

  • Amplitude. Larger A raises Q and lowers noise, but enlarges the smallest visible object and can cause TM nanoshaving. Too small (≈10 nm in liquid) makes imaging unstable. The authors used 20–800 nm in air and 10–60 nm in liquid.
  • Setpoint. Clean, hard samples: SP close to SP₀. Soft matter in liquid: start near 0.9·SP₀ and lower slightly if the image blurs. Very low SP is invasive. Ultrasharp tips may need SP around 0.95, which leaves little margin before the tip breaks.
  • Feedback. Too low blurs, too high oscillates and can wipe out phase contrast. A typical range with this setup was FB ≈ 0.1–0.5.

Tall features and image processing

The piezotube is usually centred in z after approach, so only half the range is free. For tall objects, level the sample first and then approach to use the full z range. Subtracting a smoothed copy of the data improves contrast. Tip-shape estimation can partly reconstruct the true shape.

Keeping the tip in shape

Tips that hit the surface either get sharper (loss of the double tip) or blunter. To clean a contaminated tip, use UV/ozone, piranha solution, H₂/O₂/Ar plasma, or CO₂ snow.

Glossary

SP, SP₀, A, A₀

A is the oscillation amplitude while scanning, A₀ the free amplitude near the surface. SP = A/A₀. SP₀ is the largest SP that still gives a true image. Above it, the tip loses contact.

TM and CM

Tapping mode (oscillating tip, intermittent contact) and contact mode (tip held against the surface).

FB (feedback gain)

How quickly the piezo responds to changes in height.

Q factor

Sharpness of the cantilever resonance. Low in liquid (< 10), higher in air and vacuum.

MCD, FBS, AHAPS

Monocrystalline diamond; fetal bovine serum (albumin, fibronectin, vitronectin); N-(6-aminohexyl)aminopropyltrimethoxysilane, an amino-silane linker.

Nanoshaving

Using the tip to remove a thin layer (cut a hole in CM, then image in TM) to measure layer height.
