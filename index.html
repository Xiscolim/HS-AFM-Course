Principle of AFM: an introduction for biologists

How an atomic force microscope forms an image in solution, what limits its resolution, and how the main imaging modes differ. This page summarises the opening chapter of a book on high-speed AFM (HS-AFM), written for master's and PhD students in biology.

The basic idea

AFM belongs to the family of scanning probe microscopies (SPM). An SPM brings a tiny probe close to a surface, detects a change in some physical quantity (electric current, light intensity, magnetic or mechanical force), and repeats this at many lateral positions to build a 2D map. AFM is the most widely used SPM because it is versatile and simple.

The probe is a microfabricated cantilever with a fine tip at its free end. When the tip interacts with the sample, the cantilever's behaviour changes, and that change tells you the interaction strength. While the sample stage scans sideways under the tip, a feedback loop moves the Z-position of the stage so that the interaction strength stays at a preset level. The Z-movements at each lateral position are then the topography of the sample.

In one sentence: the image is the record of how much the Z-scanner had to move to keep the tip–sample interaction constant.

1. Image formation and spatial resolution

The tip is assumed to touch the sample at its very end

In solution the tip is in contact with the sample. In reality it can touch with its end or with its side (Fig. 1.1a), but AFM always assumes contact at the end point. The height of that end point is recorded as the sample height at that (x, y). The image is therefore a convolution of the real topography with the tip shape, so it always deviates from the true surface. A sharper tip reduces the deviation.

Deconvolution and pseudo-AFM images

If the tip shape is known, the true surface can be recovered from the image by deconvolution, except in regions the tip could not reach. In practice, tip shape is not measured routinely, so this is rarely done.

The same principle is useful in the other direction. From a protein's atomic structure you can calculate a pseudo-AFM image by convolving it with a tip model. Comparing pseudo-images at different orientations with real images helps you work out how a protein is oriented and which bumps correspond to which parts of the structure. The chapter cites the BioAFMviewer software for this (Amyot & Flechsig, 2020).

Objects look wider than they are

Let Rs be the sample's radius of curvature and Rt the tip's. If the sample is as small as the tip or smaller (Rs ≤ Rt), its lateral size appears larger by a factor of:

2 √(Rt / Rs)

If the sample is larger than the tip (Rs > Rt), the enlargement is about (1 + sin θ) / cos θ, where 2θ is the cone angle of the tip. The top height of an object is measured accurately regardless of tip radius. The depth of dimples and gaps is not, because the tip may not reach the bottom.

What does "lateral resolution" mean in AFM?

It cannot be defined uniquely, and the enlargement factor above is not a measure of it. The chapter proposes a practical definition: the smallest diameter of two identical touching spheres that can still be told apart in the image (Fig. 1.3c). This gives approximately:

resolution ≈ 2 × √(2 Zmin Rt)

where Zmin is the smallest detectable height change (the Z-resolution), valid when Zmin ≪ Rt. With Zmin = 0.1 nm and Rt = 5 nm, the resolution is about 2 nm. Pixel size also limits resolution. Computing finer pixel positions by extrapolating the data can improve the apparent resolution, but this is not recommended.

Caution with 2D crystals. Estimating resolution from the Fourier transform of an image of a 2D crystal tends to give a better value than reality. Where the tip cannot reach the dimples, its path contains spatial frequencies higher than those of the sample itself (Fig. 1.4).

2. The AFM system

Most biological AFM uses amplitude modulation (AM). The cantilever is oscillated at or near its first resonant frequency, and its oscillation amplitude is held constant by feedback. A constant amplitude means a constant tip–sample force (or constant energy loss). The other modes use essentially the same hardware.

  1. Cantilever and excitation. A piezo actuator next to the cantilever makes it oscillate.
  2. Deflection detection. A laser is focused on the free end of the cantilever and the reflected beam falls on a two- or four-segment photodetector. The voltage difference between the top and bottom halves (V1 − V2, or the normalised (V1 − V2)/(V1 + V2)) is proportional to the deflection. This optical beam deflection (OBD) method is popular because it is simple and easy to use, not because it is the most sensitive. Interferometers are more sensitive for conventional cantilevers but difficult to use. Self-sensing piezoresistive cantilevers are too stiff for biological samples.
  3. Amplitude detector. Converts deflection into a voltage VA representing the oscillation amplitude.
  4. Feedback controller. Compares VA with the set point VA0 (the amplitude to be maintained). The error signal drives a PID controller.
  5. Z-scanner. Moves vertically to minimise the error. The controller output is also recorded by the computer to build the topographic image.
  6. X- and Y-scanners. Driven by the computer to scan the stage laterally.

Scanner layouts vary: the stage may scan in 3D, or the stage may scan laterally while the cantilever base moves in Z, or the cantilever may do all the scanning. The chapter assumes the stage scans in 3D. The optimisation of these components for HS-AFM is covered in a later chapter of the book.

3. Imaging modes

Imaging modes fall into three groups: DC modes, AC modes, and force–distance (FD) curve-based modes, plus the special case of recognition imaging.

DC mode (contact mode)

The cantilever is not oscillated, so the tip is always in contact. Cantilever deflection Δz gives the vertical force as kc × Δz. The cantilever is soft vertically but stiff laterally, so scanning drags the sample sideways. Samples taller than about 1 nm are easily damaged or swept away. DC mode is therefore no longer used for biological samples in solution, except for very flat ones.

AC modes in liquid

In AC mode the cantilever oscillates near its first resonance and touches the sample only briefly. There are three variants, named after the quantity held constant by feedback: AM (amplitude), PM (phase relative to the excitation) and FM (resonant frequency shift).

What water does to the cantilever

Immersed in water, the cantilever's resonant frequency falls, mainly because it drags a mass of water along with it (the rest is viscous damping). For the short cantilevers used in HS-AFM, the resonant frequency in water is about one third of that in air, and the quality factor Qc is only about 1.5. The oscillation is therefore strongly damped and the resonance peak is broad.

What the tip–sample interaction does

The tip–sample force acts like an extra spring and an extra damper. A force gradient k = ∂Fts/∂z effectively changes the cantilever's spring constant from kc to (kc − k), which shifts the resonance:

Δω0 ≈ −(ω0 / 2kc) · k

In the attractive regime k > 0, so the resonance moves to lower frequency. In the repulsive regime k < 0, so it moves to higher frequency (Fig. 1.6). In solution the attractive force is often very weak. This shift is an elastic collision and costs no energy. Damping by the sample, in contrast, dissipates energy and reduces the amplitude.

Why phase and frequency signals are tricky in liquid

The common way of exciting the cantilever is a piezo actuator beside it, which shakes the cantilever through the surrounding liquid. The time delay of that transmission depends on the liquid volume and on liquid–solid and liquid–air interfaces that change during an experiment. So the phase relative to the excitation tends to drift. Alternatives are photothermal excitation with an AC laser, or a magnetic field acting on a magnetically coated cantilever. They work, but they complicate the instrument and limit the choice of cantilevers.

What phase contrast means

In AM-AFM the amplitude is held constant, so phase contrast appears only where energy dissipation differs across the sample. This points to regions with different adhesion, viscoelasticity or friction. It also depends on the tip surface. For biological samples in solution imaged gently with conventional cantilevers, phase contrast rarely appears. Phase can be recorded in AM-AFM as a second channel if the instrument has two output channels, while amplitude stays the feedback signal.

Force–distance (FD) curve-based AFM

Also known as PeakForce tapping or off-resonance tapping. The cantilever chip is moved up and down relative to the sample sinusoidally, at frequencies well below any resonance. At each pixel the tip approaches until it pushes with the preset peak force, then retracts. The deflection gives the force directly (F = kc × Δz).

  • Topography comes from the Z-position at which the peak force is reached.
  • Elasticity is estimated from the approach curve while in contact.
  • Adhesion comes from the retraction curve.
  • Advantage: the repulsive force is precisely controlled and several properties are mapped at once. In AC modes the force cannot be controlled this precisely.
  • Disadvantages: DC deflection is measured, so mechanical drift of the detector and cantilever affects it. It is also slower than AM-AFM because the Z-motion is far below resonance.

To speed this up, a sinusoidally power-modulated blue laser can bend a short cantilever photothermally (resonance ~500 kHz in water). The heating effect acts as a low-pass filter, so reaching a ~20 nm deflection requires lowering the drive frequency to about 20 kHz, and more amplitude means more laser power and a warmer solution around the cantilever.

Recognition imaging (TREC)

Topography alone cannot tell you which species is which on a membrane fragment or cell surface. The first approach was to tether a "bait" molecule (a ligand or antibody) to the tip with a flexible linker and look for unbinding events in many force curves, which is very slow. Topography and recognition (TREC) imaging captures both images simultaneously at nearly the speed of AM imaging.

The trick is to split the oscillation into two halves. The amplitude of the lower half (Alower) is used for feedback and gives the topography. The amplitude of the upper half (Aupper) drops when the tethered ligand binds a receptor and the linker stretches, and this gives the recognition map.

TREC detects only one species at a time. It has been used to find receptors and ion channels on cell surfaces and binding sites on proteins and DNA. The parameters to tune are:

  • Cantilever Q low, or excitation below resonance, to limit crosstalk between the two images.
  • Free amplitude longer than the linker, but not so large that the vertical force breaks the bond.
  • Linker length short enough for a high effective ligand concentration, long enough to bind even when the cantilever swings up.
  • Scan speed low enough for binding to occur, but not slower than needed.

Check yourself

Why are objects wider in AFM images than in reality?

The image is a convolution of the sample with the tip. The tip's side touches the object before its end is above it, and the end height is recorded as the sample height at that position.

Can you measure the height of a small particle accurately even with a blunt tip?

Yes. The top height is measured accurately independently of tip radius. The width is overestimated, and the depth of narrow gaps is underestimated.

Estimate the lateral resolution for Zmin = 0.1 nm and Rt = 5 nm.

2 × √(2 × 0.1 × 5) = 2 × 1 = 2 nm.

Why is contact (DC) mode avoided for proteins in solution?

The cantilever is stiff laterally, so scanning drags and damages samples taller than about 1 nm.

Why is AM mode preferred over PM and FM?

It is simple and stable, disturbs the sample little, and has no limit on oscillation amplitude. PM signals are unstable in liquid and do not represent interaction strength, and FM needs very small amplitude and three controllers.

What does phase contrast tell you in AM-AFM?

That different regions of the sample dissipate energy differently, which suggests differences in adhesion, viscoelasticity or friction. It is not simply a height map.

How does TREC get topography and recognition at the same time?

The lower half of the oscillation amplitude drives the feedback (topography). The upper half is reduced by ligand–receptor binding (recognition).
