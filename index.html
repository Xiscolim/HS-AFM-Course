01 / The central idea

HS-AFM is a coordinated system.

Imagine following the contour of a soft protein with a tiny, rapidly oscillating probe. The instrument must sense a change, calculate a correction, and move vertically before the probe has travelled too far. A fast cantilever helps only if the detectors, controller, scanner, and driver can keep up.

01 · CANTILEVER

Sense the interaction

A small beam with a sharp tip oscillates in liquid. Contact changes its motion. Low stiffness helps limit disturbance; high resonance and low damping time help it respond rapidly.

§4.1 · pp. 37–42
02 · OPTICAL DETECTOR

Read the beam

Optical beam deflection (OBD) measures changes in the angle of reflected laser light. A tightly focused beam is needed because the cantilever is only a few micrometres wide.

§4.2 · pp. 42–44
03 · AMPLITUDE DETECTOR

Measure each oscillation

Amplitude is the size of the oscillation. HS-AFM needs this information within about one cycle or faster; averaging many cycles would delay the correction.

§4.3 · pp. 44–46
04 · FEEDBACK CONTROLLER

Decide the correction

The measured amplitude is compared with a target, Aₛ. The controller changes tip–sample separation to reduce the difference. This is a control loop, not direct force measurement.

§§4.6–4.7 · pp. 61–65
05 · SCANNER + DRIVER

Move precisely

Piezoelectric actuators turn electrical signals into tiny displacements. X–Y scans across the surface; Z follows height. The driver must supply these changing signals quickly enough.

§4.5 · pp. 49–60
06 · STABILITY CONTROLS

Keep the movie trustworthy

Drift compensation maintains consistent interaction conditions. Vibration damping and X–Y corrections reduce ringing and geometric distortion.

§§4.8–4.10 · pp. 66–78
For a biologist: an apparent change in molecular shape can come from the molecule, the probe–sample interaction, or the instrument’s response. Understanding the instrument helps you separate these explanations.

02 / Small cantilevers, fast responses

“Fast” and “stiff” are different properties.

The spring constant, k, describes how strongly the cantilever resists bending. Resonance frequency, fc, describes how rapidly it oscillates. Reducing its size and mass makes it possible to combine high frequency with low stiffness. The chapter also explains why short levers improve optical sensitivity.

Three terms worth knowing

fc — resonance in liquid
Use the resonance measured in the imaging liquid. An air specification is not the same quantity.
Q — quality factor
Describes how persistent the oscillation is. A smaller Q means the amplitude settles more quickly after a disturbance, at a fixed frequency.
τ — amplitude response time
A characteristic response time, not the time needed to acquire a frame.

τ = Q / (πfc)

Chapter §4.1.1, pp. 37–38. The chapter’s example uses Q ≈ 1.5 and fc ≈ 0.67 MHz in water, giving τ ≈ 0.72 μs. Its air-to-water ratio is an example, not a universal conversion.

03 / Getting the signal right

Detect quickly. Interpret carefully.

Excitation is not detection

Excitation drives the cantilever; detection reads its motion. Acoustic excitation can produce multiple peaks from the surrounding mechanics and liquid. The chapter describes using the thermal-noise spectrum to locate the small cantilever’s resonance.

Photothermal excitation can give a cleaner response, but heating and thermal expansion require attention. Likewise, increasing the detection laser power may improve the signal while adding heat.

§§4.1.4–4.2 · pp. 41–44

Phase is not a chemical label

Phase describes the timing of the cantilever’s oscillation relative to its drive. Both energy-dissipating and conservative interactions can affect it; the timing of phase detection within a cycle also matters.

In the chapter’s polymer example, adhesion and stiffness contribute differently. A bright phase region alone therefore cannot establish that a biological molecule is more hydrophobic, stiffer, or chemically different.

§4.4 · pp. 47–49
Optional detail: three ways to measure amplitude

Peak-hold: uses maxima and minima; updates every half-cycle but is more susceptible to noise. Fourier: uses sine and cosine components integrated over a cycle; provides a less noisy signal. Trigonometric: combines quadrature signals and can have a quarter-cycle conversion time in the implementation described.

These are detector timings—not whole-system response times. See §4.3, pp. 44–46.

04 / Tracking a fragile surface

The difficult part is going downhill.

The free amplitude A₀ is measured without tip–sample contact. The setpoint Aₛ is the target during imaging. A setpoint close to A₀ permits a small amplitude reduction, but leaves little error signal when the tip loses contact on a descending feature.

01 · THE PROBLEM

Tip parachuting

The surface drops away faster than the feedback can follow. The tip temporarily stops tracking it. A delayed descent can appear as a tail or broadened downhill feature.

02 · THE TEMPTATION

Increase every gain?

Stronger feedback everywhere can create overshoot and instability when climbing the next feature. Faster correction is useful only if the loop remains stable.

03 · THE SOLUTION

Dynamic PID

The controller boosts the effective error when amplitude exceeds a threshold near the setpoint, then returns to normal operation. This speeds recovery in downhill regions.

§§4.6–4.7 · pp. 61–65; Fig. 4.24 compares conventional and dynamic control using a mock AFM system.
A setpoint ratio is not a force calibration. r = Aₛ/A₀ describes relative amplitude. The same ratio does not guarantee the same interaction force across different cantilevers or imaging conditions. The chapter’s bandwidth advantage at r ≈ 0.90–0.95 belongs to its tested conditions, not a universal operating recipe.
Why doesn’t a faster cantilever guarantee a faster movie?

The total loop includes cantilever response, amplitude conversion, scanner motion, controller delay, and other delays. Surface height relative to free amplitude also affects tracking. Feedback bandwidth describes how quickly the loop can follow a varying input; it is different from cantilever resonance and from frame rate.

For a fixed image size and line count, scanning faster increases the demand on this loop. Chapter §4.7, pp. 62–65.

05 / Stability over space and time

A stable movie needs more than speed.

ChallengeWhat the chapter explainsWhy it matters for biology
Excitation driftThe free amplitude can change as excitation efficiency or liquid conditions change. The height controller may interpret this as a contact change.An image can fade even though the molecules have not disappeared. (§4.8)
Scanner vibrationFast motion can excite mechanical resonances. Momentum balancing, passive damping, and active Q-control suppress different contributions.Repeated stripes or oscillations can be instrumental rather than molecular texture. (§4.9)
Wide-area scanningLarger travel commonly requires larger or amplified mechanisms, lowering resonance and increasing coupling and nonlinearity.Moving from isolated proteins to larger biological structures changes the speed–range compromise. (§4.5)
Hysteresis and crosstalkPiezo motion need not scale linearly with voltage; motion in one axis can induce motion in another.Lengths and angles can be distorted. Calibration and compensation matter for quantitative images. (§4.10)
Two drift-compensation strategies—and an important difference

Second-harmonic approach: tracks an interaction-related signal at about twice the drive frequency and adjusts excitation. It stabilizes interaction conditions but does not directly maintain a constant A₀.

Lift-off approach: briefly separates tip and sample during retrace, measures A₀, and adjusts excitation. The chapter notes that this method does not compensate cantilever DC-deflection drift. Both use a slower correction than the main height loop. (§4.8, pp. 66–67.)

How scanner engineering helps

Dual-stage Z motion: combines a slow actuator with long travel and a fast actuator with short travel. Their calibrated motions must be combined to reconstruct height.

Q-control: reduces scanner ringing. In the chapter’s example, reducing Q from 28 to 0.7 at approximately 211 kHz reduced the reported characteristic settling time from 39 to 1 μs. This is a scanner example, not a cantilever specification.

Rounded scan turnarounds and feedforward: shape the X drive to avoid exciting resonances. These changes can reduce scan-related stripes. (§4.5.5 and §4.9.)

06 / From mechanism to diagnosis

Before calling it biology, check the instrument.

The checks below are teaching suggestions derived from the chapter’s mechanisms. A symptom can have several causes; these are not definitive diagnoses or instrument-specific settings.

A tail appears behind a raised feature

Possible mechanism: Feedback lag or parachuting may delay downhill tracking.

Useful check: Compare a slower scan and, where available, trace/retrace and amplitude-error signals. Check feedback stability rather than simply increasing all gains.

Source mechanism: §§4.6–4.7
The image gradually fades during a movie

Possible mechanism: Excitation drift can reduce A₀ toward Aₛ and cause contact loss.

Useful check: Check free amplitude according to your instrument procedure and examine drift compensation and changes in liquid conditions. Do not immediately conclude that the sample dissociated.

Source mechanism: §4.8
Regular stripes appear across unrelated objects

Possible mechanism: Scanner resonances can introduce periodic patterns.

Useful check: Check whether the pattern changes with scan rate, and inspect scanner damping and mechanical mounting with the instrument specialist.

Source mechanism: §4.9
A grid looks stretched or skewed

Possible mechanism: Hysteresis can distort displacement; X–Y crosstalk can distort angles.

Useful check: Use an appropriate calibration grating and inspect the instrument’s scale, nonlinearity, and crosstalk corrections.

Source mechanism: §4.10
Amplitude changes before expected tip contact

Possible mechanism: Liquid squeezed between the beam and surface can damp oscillation.

Useful check: Consider beam–surface separation and tip length; amplitude loss is not necessarily all due to tip contact.

Source mechanism: §4.1.3
A phase image shows strong contrast

Possible mechanism: Adhesion, conservative interactions, and detection timing may all contribute.

Useful check: Compare topography, tip chemistry, interaction conditions, and the phase-detection method before assigning a material property.

Source mechanism: §4.4
Keep with every dataset: cantilever identity, liquid resonance, free amplitude, setpoint, scan dimensions, pixel count, frame time, feedback settings, buffer, substrate, and processing history. This practical record helps distinguish changes in the sample from changes in measurement conditions.

07 / What to remember

Five ideas to take to the microscope.

  1. A small cantilever can be both fast and compliant.
  2. The entire feedback loop determines tracking performance.
  3. Gentle setpoints require reliable recovery from contact loss.
  4. Drift, ringing, and scanner distortion can mimic biological changes.
  5. Height and phase signals need controls before they become biological conclusions.
Quick glossary
Amplitude
The size of the cantilever oscillation.
OBD
Optical beam deflection: reading reflected-light position to detect cantilever bending angle.
PID
Proportional–integral–derivative control: correction based on present error, accumulated error, and the rate of error change.
Bandwidth
The range of signal-change frequencies a system can follow under a specified response criterion.
Piezo actuator
A positioning element that changes dimensions when electrically driven.
Feedforward
Adjusting the command in advance using a model of the expected response.
